Replacing a 14-year-old dispatch system without stopping the trucks

Tideway ran North American freight on a dispatch monolith older than most of its dispatchers. Meridian rebuilt it as a realtime, AI-routed platform — migrated live, one route type at a time, without a single hour of downtime.

The problem

A dispatch board held together by institutional memory

Tideway moves freight across 41 states out of a dispatch system first written in 2011 — a Windows desktop app talking to a single Postgres instance, extended for over a decade by whoever was on call. There was no API. Route assignment lived in three dispatchers' heads and a shared spreadsheet nobody else was allowed to touch.

It worked, in the way legacy systems work: badly, and only for the people who already knew where the bodies were buried. Growth made it worse. Every new lane Tideway added meant more manual cross-referencing — driver hours-of-service limits, equipment type, customer SLAs — done by hand, under time pressure, at 5 a.m.

The board had quietly become the company's biggest scaling risk. Three dispatchers, each with years of tribal knowledge, were a single-point-of-failure for a nine-figure logistics operation.

What made this different from a typical rewrite Tideway couldn't stop dispatching to build a better dispatch system. Trucks move 24 hours a day; a multi-day cutover was not an option on the table.

Constraints

What we weren't allowed to break

Four rules shaped every architecture decision on this engagement.

  • No downtime, ever. Freight doesn't pause for maintenance windows — the fleet runs three shifts, seven days a week.
  • Dispatchers had to trust it on day one. A system that overrides an experienced dispatcher's judgment without explanation gets ignored, not adopted.
  • Legacy telematics data was inconsistent. Six GPS vendors, six data formats, none of them clean.
  • Union scheduling rules were non-negotiable. Hours-of-service and seniority rules had to be enforced in code, exactly, with no exceptions the old system's dispatchers used to grant informally.

The approach

Strangler-fig migration, not a rewrite

We built the new system alongside the old one and moved traffic a route type at a time — the monolith was decommissioned by attrition, not by a cutover date.

Phase 1 — parallel core

An event-driven core, running silently in the background

We stood up an event bus fed by the same telematics feeds as the legacy system, normalizing six vendors' GPS formats into one schema. For the first six weeks, the new core did nothing but shadow the old system — computing routes, logging what it would have done, and comparing against what dispatchers actually chose.

Where the AI actually helps

A solver for the 95% case, a model for the rest

Most route assignment is a constraint-satisfaction problem — hours-of-service limits, equipment compatibility, customer delivery windows — and a classical solver (OR-Tools) handles it faster and more predictably than any LLM would. We only reach for a language model at the edges: disambiguating ambiguous free-text notes from customers ("dock closes early on Fridays, call ahead"), and explaining a routing decision back to a dispatcher in plain language when they ask why.

That division of labor is deliberate. The system that moves freight worth real money runs on math you can audit; the model only touches the parts where judgment, not computation, is the bottleneck.

Results

Six months post-launch

Dispatch throughput — shipments a single dispatcher can manage per shift — is up 214%, largely because the routing solver now handles the constraint-checking that used to be manual cross-referencing. Dispatchers spend their time on exceptions and customer relationships, not spreadsheet lookups.

340 shipments are re-routed automatically each day in response to traffic, weather, and driver availability changes — re-routing that simply didn't happen under the old system because no one had time to do it by hand.

"They argued us out of half the roadmap and shipped the half that mattered. Our board thinks we tripled headcount."
